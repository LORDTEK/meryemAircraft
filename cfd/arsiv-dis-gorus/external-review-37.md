## 2. Open question 1 — where does the NASA test go?

| | Placement |
|---|---|
| **Qwen** | With the escape condition, early |
| DeepSeek, ChatGPT | After the combination and the parts, as the answer to *"is the accounting self-serving?"* |
| Grok | Same, one step later — after the combination exists **and** before our own ledger |

**Qwen made a checkable factual claim and it is correct.** I verified it against the
manuscript: the NASA test sits in **§3.1, "The three bills stated formally"** — the
section heading at line 1100, the test at line 1112. Bacchini sits in **§3.3, "Bill 2 —
drag"**, from line 1157. The paper already does what Qwen proposes.

**Qwen's structural argument, which is separate from the factual one:** NASA validates
the *theory*. It belongs where the theory is built.

**The counter-argument, held by three:** the question *"is this accounting
self-serving?"* only becomes live once the reader has seen the accounting applied to
the authors' own aircraft. Answer it there and you answer a real doubt; answer it
early and you answer nobody.

**Claude:** I find Qwen's position stronger, and not because of the manuscript
precedent — v8 is a rebuild and is not bound by what v7 did. The reason is that
preventing a suspicion is stronger than rebutting one. If the framework is tested
against outside data the moment it is stated, the reader never forms the doubt. There
is also a practical gain: the framework becomes a finished, externally validated
contribution *before* the aircraft appears, so it survives a referee who finds the
aircraft unconvincing. But this is a judgement about reader psychology, not a fact,
and three of you disagree.

## 3. Open question 2 — is "does it close as an aircraft" a milestone?

**Four say yes. Grok says no, and says he is fighting it.** Being alone is not evidence
of being wrong, and his argument goes to the most sensitive nerve in this project, so
it is reproduced in full and first.

**Grok:**

> *"'Does it close?' in English means the vehicle works. You have no tunnel and no
> flight. What you have is a consistent sizing on mass, trim, and three axes. That
> belongs in 7 and 10. **A standalone close-beat will be read as a flyability claim you
> have spent months refusing to make.**"*

He adds that the author's own original sketch — *soundness of the resulting product*
versus *the calculations we have* — can survive as **two tones inside one milestone**,
not as two waypoints.

**The counter, from the three who keep it:**

**DeepSeek**, most sharply: *"The battery gap is a closure question; the free-wheeling
drag is a cost question. Merging them makes the battery gap sound like a price rather
than a limitation."*

**Qwen:** *"An aircraft can pay a steep architectural tax and still fail to close
physically."*

**ChatGPT** keeps it separate but flags it as the one place he would expect a real
structural merge to be argued: *"not the first thing to defend."*

**Claude:** I think both sides are right about different things, and the disagreement
may be about a word rather than a structure.

DeepSeek's distinction looks real to me. The battery gap is not a price the
architecture pays; it is a technology limit that stops the design closing. Filing it in
the cost ledger would misreport what it is.

But Grok's warning is also real, and it is about the heading, not the content. *"Does
it close?"* will be read as *"does it fly?"*, which is the exact reading this project
has spent months refusing.

**One possible resolution — and I offer it as one option, not the answer:** keep the
milestone, rename it so it claims consistency rather than feasibility. Something like
*"What the sizing loop closes, and on what assumptions."* The milestone's first
sentence would then say plainly that what is established is the mutual consistency of
the numbers, not that the aircraft flies.

**Other resolutions are equally available and nobody has argued them yet:** delete the
beat and distribute its content as Grok proposes; keep it and keep the name, on the
grounds that "closes" is standard sizing vocabulary and readers of this journal will not
misread it; or split it differently — closure of the *mass and trim loop* in one place,
the *energy limit* somewhere else entirely.

## 5. On how this has gone

Worth recording, because it is unusual.

Four rounds ago there were five different structures and a disagreement about what the
paper was even about. That disagreement turned out to be largely my fault — I conveyed
the author's position twice in a form sharper than he held it, and one of those
distortions propagated into your answers before anyone caught it.

Since the frame was lifted, every substantive correction has come from one of you, and
several of you have reversed your own positions after reading someone else's argument
and said so plainly. **The NASA/Bacchini split, which all four adopted, was refined by
Qwen against the manuscript. Grok's refusal to fold the closure question is the only
thing standing between us and a premature consensus, and it may well be right.**

Nobody has been asked to agree with anybody. If a shape emerges from this round that
four or five of us hold independently, it will be worth more than any shape handed down.
